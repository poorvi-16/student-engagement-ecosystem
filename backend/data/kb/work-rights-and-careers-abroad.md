## Part-time work is a top-up, not your funding plan
Almost every country caps student work hours, and visa officers want to see that your tuition and living costs are covered without it. Typical rules: the USA allows on-campus jobs for limited hours during term; the UK usually allows up to 20 hours a week in term time on a degree course; Canada allows off-campus work for eligible students with a weekly cap during term; Australia uses a fortnightly hour cap; Germany counts days per year; Ireland and the Netherlands also cap weekly hours with more freedom in holidays. Exact limits change, so confirm them on the official page for your visa before you plan around them.

## Working after the degree
Post-study routes differ a lot. The USA has OPT (and a STEM extension for eligible degrees), Canada has the Post-Graduation Work Permit for eligible programs, the UK has the Graduate route, Australia has the Temporary Graduate visa, Germany has an 18-month job-search residence permit, Ireland has a Stay Back option, and the Netherlands has a one-year orientation (search year) permit. Eligibility depends on the program, the institution and the level of degree, so check this before you choose a course, not after.

## Internships, co-ops and placements
Programs with co-op terms, mandatory internships or industry projects give you local experience and references, which employers value more than the degree name alone. Ask each department how many students got internships in the last year and where.

## Building a career case early
Start LinkedIn and a one-page CV in the first month, attend career-service workshops, and join one or two student clubs related to your field. Keep your visa conditions in mind for any job: unpaid or self-employed work is often not allowed.

## Staying honest in interviews
Visa interviews ask what you will do after the degree. A specific plan (roles, companies, skills you will build) is more credible than "I will see". Never say you plan to work illegally or rely on part-time work to pay fees.
