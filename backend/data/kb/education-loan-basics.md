## Who lends for higher education
Public and private banks, and NBFCs (non-banking financial companies) that specialise in education loans. Banks are usually cheaper for secured loans but slower and stricter on collateral; NBFCs are faster, lend more without collateral for well-ranked universities and courses, and charge higher interest. All are regulated by the RBI and must disclose the interest rate, fees and APR upfront under fair-practice rules.

## What a loan covers
Tuition and university fees, living costs, travel, health insurance, books, a laptop and caution deposits. Lenders usually pay tuition directly to the university and release living costs in instalments.

## Secured vs unsecured loans
A secured loan is backed by collateral, usually residential property, a flat, non-agricultural land, fixed deposits or LIC policies. It gives higher amounts and lower rates. An unsecured loan needs no collateral but depends heavily on the university and course ranking, your academics and the co-applicant's income; limits are lower and rates higher. Many NBFCs keep lists of "approved" universities with different unsecured limits by tier.

## Co-applicant (guarantor)
Almost every education loan needs a co-applicant, usually a parent, spouse, sibling or close relative with a stable income and a good credit (CIBIL) score. The co-applicant is equally responsible for repayment. A higher co-applicant income and a clean repayment history improve the amount and rate offered.

## Interest rate, fees and APR
Rates are floating or fixed and are quoted as a margin over the lender's benchmark rate. Expect a processing fee (often 0.5–2% of the loan amount) and sometimes insurance or documentation charges. Compare offers on APR (the all-in yearly cost including fees), not only the headline rate. Women applicants and students at top universities often get small concessions.

## Moratorium and repayment
Repayment usually starts after a moratorium of course duration plus 6–12 months. Interest accrues during the moratorium (often simple interest); paying it monthly while studying, even partly, reduces the final EMI. After the moratorium the loan is repaid in EMIs over typically 10–15 years. Prepayment is usually allowed, often without penalty on floating-rate loans.

## Margin money
Some lenders expect the family to fund a small share of the cost (margin money, often 0–15%), especially on larger secured loans.

## How lenders check affordability (FOIR)
Lenders look at the co-applicant's FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio): existing EMIs plus any EMI they would pay, divided by monthly income. Many lenders want this below roughly 50–60%. They also look at the student's expected post-study salary for the course and country.

## Government schemes for studying in India
PM-Vidyalaxmi (launched 2024) offers collateral-free, guarantor-free loans to students admitted to top-ranked Indian institutions, with a government credit guarantee and an interest subvention for families below an income limit. The Central Sector Interest Subsidy scheme covers moratorium interest for economically weaker families. Applications go through the Vidya Lakshmi portal.
